# Why Computers Hate Bank Statements

## Hidden Paper Costs
[visual: person holding paper statement]
You have probably scanned a stack of bank papers into your computer to get your taxes done on time. If that digital file fails to match your actual balance, you might lose money on fees. Most people assume that reading words off a page is simple for a modern machine. They think the software just looks at the ink and translates it directly into numbers.

## The Messy Page
[visual: close up of bank logo]
That obvious answer is completely wrong because bank pages were never built for machines to read. They were designed by humans for humans who understand context, layout, and financial jargon at a glance. When a computer looks at the same document, it sees a chaotic puzzle of loose grids, stray lines, and overlapping fonts. The page layout changes every month, meaning a script cannot rely on a fixed position to find your data.

## Visual Traps
[visual: finger pointing at small print]
Rows of transactions often lack vertical lines to separate the columns neatly. A description of a coffee shop might wrap onto a second line right into the amount column. This fools the software into thinking the purchase price is part of the store name. Without a clear border, the reading tool has no reliable way to know where one data field ends and another begins.

## Font Chaos
[visual: magnifying glass over text]
Banks love to use wildly different font sizes and weights within the same single paragraph. A merchant name might be bold, while the date sits in a tiny, faint grey font. Some numbers use commas where others use periods, depending on which country issued the account. The software gets confused by these tiny typographical shifts and skips entire lines of history.

## Floating Totals
[visual: accountant typing on calculator]
Footer notes and promotional text sit right in the middle of the transaction columns. A marketing banner about a new credit card can look identical to a debit entry to an unmanaged parser. The algorithm reads straight across the page, crashing right through disclaimers and legal warnings that happen to share the same horizontal space. Every stray graphic element acts like a trap for the reading logic.

## Broken Words
[visual: printer rolling out paper]
A long merchant name runs out of room and finishes on the line below, with no hyphen to mark the break. Your eye joins the two halves on the printed page without even noticing. The machine does not. It reads half a name, finds nothing that matches it, and leaves the entry blank. You get fragments in your ledger, and you retype them by hand off the paper.

## Midnight Fees
[visual: stressed person at desk]
This failure bites you hard when a mortgage lender rejects your digital income verification because the automated parser mangled your deposit history. You miss out on a house purchase because a script could not tell the difference between a fee and a transfer. The bank's computer spits out an error flag, and a human loan officer has to review the whole file by hand.

## Manual Checking
[visual: hands flipping through ledger]
Stop trusting raw PDF exports to feed your personal finance apps automatically. Always open the raw document and check every single line against your receipts before you submit anything. Type out the missing entries yourself when the tool stumbles on a weird font. Taking ten minutes to verify the text saves you hours of accounting headaches later.

## Final Truth
[visual: closed laptop on desk]
Bank statements defeat machines because they were drawn for human eyes, not algorithms.
